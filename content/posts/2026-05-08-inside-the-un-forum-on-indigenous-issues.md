From April 20 to May 1, 2026, the **25th session of the United Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues (UNPFII)** met at UN Headquarters in New York. The Forum is a high-level advisory body to the UN Economic and Social Council and the most important platform for Indigenous peoples to speak directly to the UN and its member states.

This year's session drew thousands of participants: Indigenous peoples, member-state delegations, UN agencies and academics. UN Secretary-General António Guterres, General Assembly President Annalena Baerbock and ECOSOC President Lok Bahadur Thapa addressed the opening.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/unpfii/secretary-general-opening.jpg" alt="Secretary-General António Guterres addressing the opening of the Forum in the General Assembly Hall" loading="lazy"><figcaption>UN Secretary-General António Guterres speaks at the opening ceremony.</figcaption></figure>

Indigenous peoples make up about 6% of the world's population yet protect around 40% of its biodiversity. Over 25 years, the Forum has pushed Indigenous issues into international debate and won wider recognition of Indigenous rights around the world.

This session's agenda was broad, with a core focus on **the holistic health of Indigenous peoples**. Participants stressed that Indigenous health is inseparable from land, water, language, culture and ecosystems, and that conflict and the climate crisis are making those links ever more fragile. Beyond health, the Forum examined climate threats, green jobs and the transition of traditional pastoralism, aiming to build comprehensive protection of Indigenous rights through institutional innovation and policy implementation.

## <span class="h-num">01</span> Two global conversations on one day

April 20 was not only the start of a high-level dialogue on Indigenous issues. It was also **UN Chinese Language Day**.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/unpfii/un-chinese-language-day.jpg" alt="UN Chinese Language Day banner, April 20" loading="lazy"></figure>

Held on *Guyu* ("Grain Rain") in honor of Cangjie, the legendary creator of Chinese characters, Chinese Language Day carried extra weight in 2026: the 80th anniversary of the UN's Chinese translation service. From the Chinese signature on the UN Charter in 1945 to its place today as one of the world's most widely used official languages, Chinese has been part of the UN's story for more than 80 years.

UN Headquarters marked the day with a series of events showcasing Chinese as one of the UN's six official languages. That evening, the curved corridor of the Secretariat Building hosted Tang-poetry recitation, classical dance, movable-type printing and lantern painting.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/unpfii/sun-lei-remarks.jpg" alt="Ambassador Sun Lei speaking at the Chinese Language Day event" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Sun Lei, Deputy Permanent Representative of China to the UN, speaks at the Chinese Language Day event.</figcaption></figure>

On the same historic day, **a global platform for Indigenous voices and an international celebration of Chinese culture** met at the United Nations: a multilingual, multicultural conversation. WinPlus students and teachers were in the room. Over the following days they watched the world listen to marginalized Indigenous voices, and began to understand what a truly international perspective means.

## <span class="h-num">03</span> An opening that set the tone

The opening ceremony began unforgettably: a representative of the Indigenous cultures of North America's Northwest Coast, wearing a traditional woven cedar hat and holding a wooden rhythm instrument, walked the hall singing. In Indigenous cultures, a ceremonial song like this opens a space, welcomes those who arrive, and reconnects people with land, culture and memory.

In his opening address, Secretary-General Guterres called Indigenous peoples "guardians of nature" and keepers of the cultures and knowledge that sustain human civilization. He urged states to turn the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples into national laws and policies, and to ensure Indigenous peoples' "full and meaningful participation" in decisions at every level. He also called urgently for decisive action to protect Indigenous leaders and human-rights defenders from violence and risk.

<div class="callout-dark"><p class="big">Real respect isn't speaking for others.</p><p class="small">It means breaking down structural exclusion so Indigenous peoples are truly heard, deeply involved, and hold real decision-making power on every issue that shapes their lives and our shared future.</p></div>

## <span class="h-num">04</span> A hall full of stories

Indigenous representatives from around the world, in traditional dress, turned the General Assembly Hall into a striking global tableau: felt hats and embroidered robes from the Central Asian steppe, shawls and long tunics from South Asia, traditional garments from the Himalayas. Every pattern, color and ornament carries a people's history, environment, values and heritage.

Cathy Guo wore traditional Yao dress from Hezhou, Guangxi, to the Forum and to that evening's Chinese Language Day celebration. **Its vivid red base, fine embroidery and layered silver ornaments showed the richness of China's many ethnic cultures.** In an international setting like this, simply presenting one's culture is itself an act of exchange and mutual respect.

## <span class="h-num">05</span> What the students took away

Teaching assistant Youran recorded her reflections during the Forum:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>"When voices from different countries, cultures and histories come together in the same room, global issues stop being concepts in a book. They become realities shared by real people."</p><cite>Youran, WinPlus teaching assistant</cite></blockquote>

At a side event on Indigenous peoples, climate change and land conflict, Youran gained a new understanding of familiar ideas. Climate change affects Indigenous peoples more directly and in more complex ways: not only the natural environment, but the long-standing relationship between people and land, their traditions and the continuity of their communities. Her view of "land" itself shifted: "For many Indigenous groups, land holds ancestral memory, tradition and spiritual belonging. When land is taken, developed or redefined, what is affected is not just living space but the foundation a people needs to carry its culture and way of life forward."

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/unpfii/reflection-youran.jpg" alt="Indigenous representatives in traditional dress at UN Headquarters" loading="lazy"></figure>

**One student**, a Hakka from Fujian, found an unexpected resonance:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>"When these languages are forgotten, what's lost isn't just a tool for communication. It's a history, an identity and a deep connection between people and their land."</p><cite>A WinPlus student</cite></blockquote>

That took her beyond an abstract idea of "cultural preservation" to the heart of rights and dignity. "It isn't only about offering support. We need to listen to Indigenous peoples themselves and let them lead," she said. "Only when Indigenous peoples can express their own needs and shape their own development do protections really mean something." Indigenous issues stopped being "someone else's problem" and became a question of rights, participation, dignity and cultural continuity.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/unpfii/reflection-vivian.jpg" alt="A WinPlus student holding a Forum publication in the General Assembly Hall" loading="lazy"></figure>

**Another student** noticed something important: in global education, young people often know little about Indigenous history, culture and current realities, far less than about other global issues. If young people aren't taught to understand Indigenous rights, she asked, how will these problems ever get the attention they need? Education and awareness, she concluded, are the foundation of real change, and young people have a responsibility to break the silence through learning, conversation and advocacy.

## <span class="h-num">06</span> Why we bring students to the UN

WinPlus keeps bringing students to United Nations meetings because of what we believe about education. In a globalized world, knowledge alone is not enough. Students need a **whole-person education**: not only subject knowledge, but critical thinking, cross-cultural understanding and the ability to take part in international dialogue. The soft skills they need (expression, listening and collaboration) are best built in real international settings.

Above all, WinPlus is committed to developing students' **global competency**: not only understanding global issues, but engaging in global dialogue responsibly, as a new generation of leaders for educational equity and impact.

At the UN, students saw diverse cultures coexist, heard marginalized voices, and reflected on power and participation. That kind of learning goes deeper and lasts longer than any lecture.

In the General Assembly we saw more than a debate on Indigenous issues. We saw how a fairer, more inclusive global order is built, one voice and one honest conversation at a time. By taking part, WinPlus students are becoming witnesses to and participants in that process, learning not only what "Indigenous rights" means but how to approach a complex, diverse world with respect, openness and critical thinking. That may be the most important value of education in the age of AI.
