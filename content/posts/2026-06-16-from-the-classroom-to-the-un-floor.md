On June 10, 2026, the 19th session of the Conference of States Parties to the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities was under way at United Nations Headquarters in New York. In the conference room, a 17-year-old Chinese high school student was waiting for his turn to speak.

Delegates from around the world took the floor one after another. When his turn came, the chair announced: "Next, we invite the NGO representative from China."

In a dark suit, he sat at the speaker's desk. The microphone light came on, and he began a statement of just over two minutes: how his father had shaped him, where the limits of medicine lie, the visible and invisible barriers his disabled peers meet at school, and, finally, the responsibility of his generation.

<blockquote class="pull"><p>A doctor's hands can save one life at a time. An inclusive society can change the lives of millions.</p><cite>From his statement to the conference</cite></blockquote>

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/un-crpd-speech/un-web-tv-broadcast.jpg" alt="UN Web TV broadcast showing the student speaking at the NGO desk" loading="lazy"><figcaption>His statement as broadcast live on UN Web TV.</figcaption></figure>

Applause filled the room. Two minutes is not long, but it meant a 17-year-old's voice was formally recorded in a United Nations meeting. Behind those minutes were days of thinking and preparation, and a mentor team at his side throughout.

Disability issues come down to whether a person can go to school, work and access information on equal terms, and take part in the decisions that affect them. For a student still learning about the world, coming close to those realities is also a lesson in understanding difference, respecting other people's choices and asking what you can do for others.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/photos/cosp19-student-guo-un-emblem.webp" alt="The student and Cathy beside the UN emblem and flag at UN Headquarters" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The student with WinPlus co-founder Cathy at UN Headquarters.</figcaption></figure>

Over the past three years, WinPlus students have witnessed and taken part in global conversations as they happen: from the UN Climate Change Conference to the Commission on the Status of Women, from the Asia-Pacific Forum on Sustainable Development to the ECOSOC Youth Forum. At the UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues this April, WinPlus students were there too.

This year also marks twenty years since the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities was adopted. From June 9 to 11, 2026, government delegations, international organizations, disability-rights advocates and youth representatives met at UN Headquarters for the Convention's 19th Conference of States Parties. At the session, China's candidate, Professor Chang Jian, was elected to the UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, the first Chinese member of the committee in ten years.

The theme was "The Convention at 20: consolidating achievements and charting the next phase of implementation in a changing world." Three round tables focused on ending exploitation, violence and abuse against persons with disabilities; strengthening care and support systems so people can live independently; and moving persons with disabilities from participation to representation in political and public life. All three pointed to one question: how do equal rights actually reach everyday life?

The trip was led by WinPlus co-founder <a href="../team.html#cathy-guo">Cathy Guo</a>, who previously worked at UN Women and graduated from the Harvard Kennedy School. Along the way she was more than the organizer. She was his guide:

**She secured him a rare speaking slot in a crowded session, introduced him to delegates from many countries, and connected him with a disability-rights lawyer.**

WinPlus believes the real classroom is in the real international arena.

<p class="sec-num"><span>02</span></p>

While drafting his statement, the student kept returning to one question: is disability a medical problem or a social one?

His father is a surgeon who, on his busiest days, performs seven operations. Growing up, the student saw disability through the lens of treatment and rehabilitation. But as he wrote, he realized:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>A doctor can restore a body's function, but not a person's sense of belonging in society.</p></blockquote>

At the UN, that idea became concrete. Jet-lagged, he listened from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Between sessions he sought out participants from Saudi Arabia, Japan, Spain and elsewhere to ask how their countries support people with disabilities.

During the conference, the China Disabled Persons' Federation presented *Blooming Together*, an exhibition of more than 200 works by Chinese artists with disabilities, from calligraphy and porcelain to wood carving and paper-cutting.

Cathy took him to meet one of the exhibitors, Dongyang wood-carving master Hu Xianmin, who became disabled through illness at the age of four.

<blockquote class="pull"><p>Disability is a physical limitation, but it cannot define a person's courage.</p><cite>Hu Xianmin, Dongyang wood-carving master</cite></blockquote>

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/un-crpd-speech/blooming-together-exhibition.jpg" alt="The student, Cathy and wood-carving master Hu Xianmin in front of the Blooming Together exhibition wall" loading="lazy"><figcaption>With Dongyang wood-carving master Hu Xianmin at the <em>Blooming Together</em> exhibition.</figcaption></figure>

Standing before Hu's large carving *Endless Life*, the student thought of the last line of his own statement: that everyone, whatever their abilities, should not only be cared for but truly seen. The exhibition hall was doing exactly that.

Another session stayed with him: a side event on the Deaf community and sign-language culture, where most of the speakers were Deaf and presented entirely in sign language. At the end, a Deaf speaker from Malta told, in sign, what life was like before Maltese Sign Language was legally recognized: how hard it was to get everyday information, and how often people were misunderstood.

He never said a word, yet the room was completely silent. Movement, expression and body language conveyed the helplessness, persistence and hope of a whole community. The student later wrote in his reflection:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>It was the first time I felt so directly that sign language isn't just a tool for communication. It carries Deaf people's culture, their identity, and their long fight for equal rights.</p><cite>From the student's reflection</cite></blockquote>

<p class="sec-num"><span>03</span></p>

Over his days at the UN, the student noticed one principle repeated again and again:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>Nothing About Us Without Us.</p><cite>A principle of the disability-rights movement</cite></blockquote>

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/un-crpd-speech/youth-advocate-session.jpg" alt="A conference room at UN Headquarters during a session with a youth advocate speaking" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A young advocate from New York addresses a session.</figcaption></figure>

At one session, a young man about his age took the floor. He came from an autism advocacy organization in New York and, as an autistic person and an advocate, explained clearly how people with disabilities can speak for themselves and genuinely take part in public life. Many in the room responded to him.

The moment had a big impact on the student. Traditional goodwill often casts people with disabilities as passive recipients of care. Here they were speakers, advocates and leaders of the agenda. In his reflection that day he wrote:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>This conference showed me that real equality isn't only about offering help. It's about giving everyone the chance to be respected, to be heard and to take part in shaping society.</p><cite>From the student's reflection</cite></blockquote>

<p class="sec-num"><span>04</span></p>

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/un-crpd-speech/guo-shi-student-un.jpg" alt="Cathy, Vania and the student outside UN Headquarters" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Cathy, Vania and the student at the United Nations.</figcaption></figure>

Speaking in a formal UN session requires accreditation review, document submission and a place on the speakers' list. The student started at number 153. At the usual pace, his turn would most likely never come.

Cathy went to the session's organizer, introduced him and explained that he had come from China hoping for the chance to speak. He didn't give up either: he waited in the room with his statement in hand. As some speakers ahead of him cancelled or didn't appear, his place moved up, until his two minutes finally arrived.

Before he spoke, he was visibly nervous, his face flushed red. <a href="../team.html#vania-shi">Vania Shi</a>, who traveled with him, encouraged him: "One day you're going to be a world-class doctor speaking in UN halls. You can do this little speech today."

Cathy put a hand on his shoulder: "Your thinking and your words are more than good enough. Relax and enjoy the stage."

At the microphone, he began with his childhood: why he wanted to study medicine, and why he came to see that medicine alone cannot build an inclusive world. As one of very few young speakers, he drew glances from around the room as delegates turned to see where the young voice was coming from. He closed:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>My generation will become the designers of our institutions. That responsibility doesn't have to wait for the halls of power. It starts now, in the hallways of our schools.</p><cite>The close of his statement</cite></blockquote>

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/photos/cosp19-general-assembly-hall.webp" alt="The student in the gallery of the UN General Assembly Hall" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The student in the UN General Assembly Hall.</figcaption></figure>

His mother watched the speech live on the UN's official broadcast. Afterwards she wrote in the parents' group:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>Watching today's video, he really opened up… We gained so much, and the trip was more than worth it. Special thanks to the teachers for their careful arrangements and for being with him every step of the way!</p><cite>His mother</cite></blockquote>

Cathy replied: "Walking alongside him and seeing him grow has been WinPlus's privilege. All of his teachers are proud of him."

That afternoon, through her Harvard alumni network, Cathy arranged a one-on-one video call for him with Daniel Florio, a fellow Kennedy School alumnus and a researcher and lawyer in disability rights. Drawing on his own experience, Daniel walked the student through the thinking behind international disability-rights legislation. Comparing Daniel's experience with schools today, the student could see how much progress has been made, and how much still deserves attention.

<p class="sec-num"><span>05</span></p>

After the conference, asked what he gained most from the trip, the student said:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>Being able to finish those two-plus minutes at the UN made the whole trip worth it.</p><cite>The student</cite></blockquote>

That may be the point of it all: for a teenager to feel, for real, that his voice can be heard by the world.

For years WinPlus has taken students into the rooms where things happen: from labs at Harvard and MIT, to Silicon Valley technology companies, to the halls of the United Nations. Our global mentor team brings deep international experience and backs every student to open the door to a larger world.

**In this new era, knowledge can be searched. But global competency (cross-cultural empathy, composure in real high-pressure settings, and the poise to speak as an equal with leaders in any field) can only be built, step by step, in the real arena.**

Back from New York, the student is noticeably more confident. Impressed by what he saw of the WinPlus team's credentials and support, he has chosen WinPlus to guide him through his college applications. The two-minute statement, the conversations in the conference rooms, the reflection and growth will all become part of the story he writes. And a line from his speech may be the best summary of the journey: "This responsibility starts today, in the hallways of our schools."
