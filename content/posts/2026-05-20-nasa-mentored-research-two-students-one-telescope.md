At the start of summer 2025, two 11th graders were each stuck.

**The astronomer**, an 11th grader in the international division of a top high school in China, had just been rejected by a well-known academic program, while several classmates were accepted. Sitting at his desk, he wondered for the first time whether he simply wasn't good enough.

**The engineer** was in 11th grade at a public high school in the United States with almost no research resources. Her parents were anxious about college. Her father, a classic strict parent, worried that his quiet daughter wouldn't stand up for herself and go after opportunities.

What they couldn't have known was that within weeks, in WinPlus's NASA research internship, one would become the team's "astronomer" and the other its "aerospace engineer." Like a real NASA team, they would divide the work and design a deep-space telescope together.

Even less could they have imagined that those eight weeks would reshape their applications and the direction of their education.

## <span class="h-num">01</span> Seeing past the worry

When the engineer and her parents first came to us, WinPlus co-founder Cathy Guo could feel the parents' anxiety.

"Her public school has limited resources and she's never done research," her parent said. "She doesn't have a standout project, and she's always so unhurried. She's at a real disadvantage for good schools."

But as they talked, Cathy noticed details hidden beneath the worry.

"This girl has very strong hands-on ability," Cathy recalled. "She teaches herself to knit. In her school's drama club, she sewed every one of the props on stage herself."

The engineer had strong spatial imagination and making skills. She just needed a stage to use them.

In the online assessment, Rachel didn't open with her own research. She asked the engineer a simple question: "What do you most like to do?" The engineer answered: "I like designing things, and mechanical structures."

As it happened, the astronomer was in the same interview. "I'm fascinated by astrophysics and data analysis," he said.

Rachel's eyes lit up. "You two should work together!" Instead of handing them an off-the-shelf, assembly-line project, she designed a new direction around the overlap of their interests: a NASA deep-space telescope research internship.

One student would be the team's aerospace engineer, responsible for the telescope itself. The other would be the planetary astronomer, responsible for the astrophysics. Like two closely linked departments inside NASA, each would own its part and make the other stronger. In that moment, both students' dream of space caught fire.

## <span class="h-num">03</span> The engineer: from shy to confident

The start wasn't easy for the engineer. Engineering formulas, optical design software and structural analysis tools looked like a foreign language to an 11th grader.

But Rachel's teaching was practical. "You don't need to grind through every theoretical derivation right now," she told the engineer. "In industry, what matters more is using the right tool, at the right moment, to solve the right problem."

She sent the engineer real NASA telescope design reports and taught her to work like an experienced engineer: break down the task, evaluate materials, calculate strength.

Step by step, the engineer changed. "I realized the physics I learned in class isn't just for exams," she said. "It can actually be used to design a space telescope. Putting theory into practice like that is really interesting."

<blockquote class="pull"><p>"She started out as a very shy, introverted girl. By the end, her communication skills were outstanding."</p><cite>Cedric Pan, WinPlus co-founder, who guided the project throughout</cite></blockquote>

The once-hesitant student began to look like an engineer. She went looking for telescope design drawings on her own and started asking questions: "If we switch this part to a composite, would strength and weight improve?" "If we change this structure, would we lose less light in the optical path?" She went from waiting for instructions to being a real collaborator on a real research project.

After eight weeks, the engineer delivered a professional-grade mechanical design report for a deep-space telescope and earned a recommendation letter from Rachel.

This admissions season she was **admitted to the University of Michigan's mechanical engineering program**, along with mechanical engineering offers from UCLA, UIUC and more than ten US Top-30 universities. Looking back, the research and Rachel's letter were the strongest pillars of her application.

## <span class="h-num">04</span> The astronomer: simpler is smarter

In the same team, the astronomer went through a different transformation.

He had always been a science fanatic, never without a science-fiction novel, with a deep passion for astrophysics. But just before application season, he was rejected by a well-known research program while several classmates got in.

"I was really panicking," he recalls. "Everyone else had something and I didn't. I wondered if I'd get in anywhere."

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/nasa-research/student-harvard-robotics.jpg" alt="The astronomer and other students examining robotic hands in a Harvard lab" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The astronomer (left) learning about humanoid robotics at Harvard.</figcaption></figure>

His first telescope design was elaborate: a three-mirror system with multiple layers, very impressive-looking. Rachel asked him to rethink it: "Do these complex designs actually improve performance?"

The astronomer realized something important: real research isn't about making a simple problem complicated, it's about finding the most direct path to its core. He started over, cut everything unnecessary and switched to a lean two-mirror design. Along the way he learned to use CODE V optical design software and to judge when a coronagraph is needed. These ways of thinking and tools went well beyond a high school curriculum.

More importantly, for the first time he truly "saw" the atmospheres of distant planets in cold spectral data, and used them to estimate where life might exist. He knew then what he wanted: to use astronomy to search for life beyond the solar system.

Because of that shared dream, the two students named their project **FINBOR**, short for "Finding Neighbor."

**That way of thinking, earned in real research, proved decisive in the astronomer's Cambridge interview.** The professor wrote a problem on the whiteboard that was far beyond the syllabus: derive the packing efficiency of a particular crystal structure, live. The astronomer had never studied it.

But he didn't panic. Trained by the NASA project to face the unknown, find tools and break problems down, he stayed calm. Rather than jumping to an answer, he used what he did understand about crystals to walk the professor through his chain of reasoning.

A few months later, **offers in astrophysics from Cambridge, Imperial College London and UC Berkeley** arrived.

<blockquote class="pull"><p>"I used to think grades and test scores were what mattered most. Now I understand that what matters most is finding a direction that's truly your own. Taking a different path turned out to suit me better."</p><cite>The astronomer</cite></blockquote>
