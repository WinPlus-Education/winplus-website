On June 10, 2026, the 19th session of the Conference of States Parties to the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) was under way at United Nations Headquarters in New York. In the conference room, a 17-year-old Chinese high school student was waiting for his turn.

Delegates from around the world took the floor one after another. Then the chair announced: "Next, we invite the NGO representative from China."

The student, in a dark suit, sat at the speaker's desk. The microphone light came on, and he began a statement of just over two minutes: how his father had shaped him, where the limits of medicine lie, the visible and invisible barriers his disabled peers meet at school, and, finally, the responsibility of his generation.

<blockquote class="pull"><p>"A doctor's hands can save one life at a time. An inclusive society can change the lives of millions."</p><cite>From the student's statement to COSP19</cite></blockquote>

Applause followed. Two minutes is not long, but it meant a 17-year-old's voice was formally recorded in a United Nations meeting. Behind those minutes were days of drafting and rehearsal, and a mentor team at his side throughout.

Disability issues come down to whether a person can go to school, work and access information on equal terms, and take part in the decisions that affect them. For a student still learning about the world, coming close to those realities is also a lesson in understanding difference, respecting other people's choices and asking what you can do for others.

## <span class="h-num">01</span> A convention turns twenty

Over the past three years, WinPlus students have taken part in global conversations as they happen: from the UN Climate Change Conference to the Commission on the Status of Women, from the Asia-Pacific Forum on Sustainable Development to the ECOSOC Youth Forum. In April 2026, WinPlus students also attended the UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues.

2026 marks twenty years since the CRPD was adopted. From June 9 to 11, government delegations, international organizations, disability-rights advocates and youth representatives met at UN Headquarters for the 19th session. During the conference, the Chinese candidate, Professor Chang Jian, was elected to the UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, the first Chinese member of the committee in ten years.

The theme was "The Convention at 20: consolidating achievements and charting the next phase of implementation in a changing world." Three round tables focused on ending exploitation, violence and abuse against persons with disabilities; strengthening care and support systems so that people can live independently; and moving persons with disabilities from participation to representation in political and public life. All three pointed to one question: how do equal rights actually reach everyday life?

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/photos/cosp19-student-guo-un-emblem.webp" alt="The student and Cathy Guo standing beside the UN emblem and flag at UN Headquarters" loading="lazy"><figcaption>the student with WinPlus co-founder Cathy Guo at UN Headquarters.</figcaption></figure>

The trip was led by WinPlus co-founder Cathy Guo, who previously worked at UN Women and holds a Master in Public Policy from the Harvard Kennedy School. She was more than the organizer. She was the student's guide: **securing a rare speaking slot in a crowded session, introducing him to delegates from many countries, and arranging for him to talk with a disability-rights lawyer.** WinPlus believes the real classroom is in the room where the world's issues are decided.

## <span class="h-num">02</span> A medical problem, or a social one?

While drafting his statement, the student kept returning to one question: is disability a medical problem or a social one?

His father is a surgeon who, on the busiest days, performs seven operations. Growing up, the student saw disability through the lens of treatment and rehabilitation. But as he wrote, he realized that a doctor can restore a body's function, but not a person's sense of belonging in society.

At the UN, that idea became concrete. Jet-lagged, he listened from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Between sessions he sought out participants from Saudi Arabia, Japan, Spain and elsewhere to ask how their countries support people with disabilities.

During the conference, the China Disabled Persons' Federation presented *Blooming Together*, an exhibition of more than 200 works by Chinese artists with disabilities, including calligraphy, porcelain, wood carving and paper-cutting.

Cathy Guo took the student to meet one of the exhibitors, Dongyang wood-carving master Hu Xianmin, who became disabled through illness at the age of four. "Disability is a physical limitation," he told them, "but it cannot define a person's courage."

Standing before Hu's large carving *Endless Life*, the student thought of the last line of his statement: that everyone, whatever their abilities, should not only be cared for but truly seen. The exhibition hall was doing exactly that.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/un-crpd-speech/blooming-together-exhibition.jpg" alt="The student, Cathy Guo and wood-carving master Hu Xianmin in front of the Blooming Together exhibition wall" loading="lazy"><figcaption>With Dongyang wood-carving master Hu Xianmin at the <em>Blooming Together</em> exhibition.</figcaption></figure>

Another session stayed with him: a side event on the Deaf community and sign-language culture, where most speakers were Deaf and presented entirely in sign language. At the end, a Deaf speaker from Malta told, in sign, the story of life before Maltese Sign Language was legally recognized: how hard it was to get everyday information, and how often people were misunderstood.

He never said a word, yet the room was completely silent. Movement, expression and body language conveyed the helplessness, persistence and hope of a whole community.

"It was the first time I felt so directly that sign language isn't just a tool for communication," the student wrote in his notes. "It carries Deaf people's culture, their identity, and their long fight for equal rights."

## <span class="h-num">03</span> "Nothing About Us Without Us"

Over those days, the student noticed one principle repeated again and again.

<div class="callout-dark"><p class="big">"Nothing About Us Without Us."</p><p class="small">Decisions about people with disabilities should not be made without them.</p></div>

At one session, a young man about the student's age took the floor. He was from an autism advocacy organization in New York and, as an autistic person and an advocate, he explained clearly how people with disabilities can speak for themselves and genuinely take part in public life. Many in the room responded to him.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/un-crpd-speech/youth-advocate-session.jpg" alt="A conference room at UN Headquarters during a session with a youth advocate speaking" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A young self-advocate from New York addresses a session.</figcaption></figure>

The moment had a big impact on the student. Well-meaning attitudes often cast people with disabilities as passive recipients of care. Here they were speakers, advocates and leaders of the agenda. That evening he wrote:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>"This conference showed me that real equality isn't only about offering help. It's about giving everyone the chance to be respected, to be heard and to take part in shaping society."</p><cite>the student, in his daily reflection</cite></blockquote>

## <span class="h-num">04</span> From 153rd in line to the microphone

Speaking in a formal UN session requires accreditation review, document submission and registration on a speakers' list. The student started at number 153. At the usual pace, his turn was unlikely ever to come.

Cathy Guo went to the session organizer, introduced the student and explained that he had come from China hoping for the chance to speak. The student didn't give up either: he waited in the room with his statement in hand. As some speakers ahead of him cancelled or didn't appear, his place moved up, until his two minutes finally arrived.

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/insights/un-crpd-speech/guo-shi-student-un.jpg" alt="Cathy Guo, Ms. Shi and the student outside UN Headquarters" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Cathy Guo, mentor Ms. Shi and the student at the United Nations.</figcaption></figure>

Before he spoke, the student was visibly nervous, his face flushed. Mentor Ms. Shi encouraged him: "One day you're going to be a world-class doctor speaking in UN halls. You can do this little speech today." Cathy Guo put a hand on his shoulder: "Your thinking and your words are more than good enough. Relax and enjoy the stage."

At the microphone, the student began with his childhood: why he wanted to study medicine, and why he came to see that medicine alone cannot build an inclusive world. As one of very few young speakers, he drew glances from around the room as delegates turned to see where the young voice was coming from. He closed:

<blockquote class="pull"><p>"My generation will become the designers of our institutions. That responsibility doesn't have to wait for the halls of power in the future. It starts now, in the hallways of our schools."</p><cite>The close of the student's statement</cite></blockquote>

<figure><img src="/images/uploads/photos/cosp19-general-assembly-hall.webp" alt="The student standing in the gallery of the UN General Assembly Hall" loading="lazy"><figcaption>the student in the UN General Assembly Hall.</figcaption></figure>

His mother watched the speech live on the UN's official broadcast. Afterwards she wrote in the parents' group: "Watching today's video, he really opened up... We gained so much, and the trip was more than worth it. Special thanks to the teachers for their careful arrangements and for being with him every step of the way!"

Cathy Guo replied: "Walking alongside him and seeing him grow has been WinPlus's privilege. All of his teachers are proud of him."

That afternoon, through her Harvard alumni network, Cathy Guo arranged a one-on-one video call for the student with Daniel Florio, a fellow Kennedy School alumnus and a lawyer and researcher in disability rights. Daniel walked the student through the thinking behind international disability-rights law. Comparing Daniel's experience with schools today, the student could see how much progress has been made, and how much still deserves attention.

## <span class="h-num">05</span> Why this matters

Asked afterwards what he gained most from the trip, the student said: "**Being able to finish those two-plus minutes at the UN made the whole trip worth it.**"

That may be the point of it all: for a teenager to feel, for real, that his voice can be heard by the world.

For years WinPlus has taken students into the rooms where things happen: from labs at Harvard and MIT, to Silicon Valley tech companies, to the halls of the United Nations. Our global mentor team brings deep international experience and backs every student to open the door to a larger world.

In this new era, knowledge can be searched. But global competency (cross-cultural empathy, composure under real pressure, and the poise to speak as an equal with leaders in any field) can only be built, step by step, in the real arena.

Back from New York, the student has started his college application season. The two-minute statement, the conversations in the conference rooms, the reflection and growth will all become part of the story he writes. And a line from his speech may be the best summary of the journey: "This responsibility starts today, in the hallways of our schools."
